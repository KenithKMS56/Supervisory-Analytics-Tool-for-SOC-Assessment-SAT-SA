# SAT-SA: Supervisory Analytics Tool for SOC Assessment

**SIH 2026 Idea Description · Problem statement SIH26157**

NCIIPC examiners currently judge a Critical Sector Entity's SOC by reading a small sample of its alerts and cases by hand. SAT-SA lets them read all of it. It runs offline on one workstation, takes the periodic batch the entity already submits, and hands the examiner a short, ranked list of the records most likely to show that the SOC is not working the way it reports. Every finding comes with the records behind it and a plain explanation.

We built a working prototype before writing this. Where we quote a number about SAT-SA, we measured it, and we give the date. Where we quote accuracy, it comes from synthetic data, and we say so each time.

## Context

**The problem.** NCIIPC "performs manual reviews of samples of security alerts and case-management records generated by Security Operations Centres (SOCs)" of Critical Sector Entities (CSEs). The problem statement is clear that the point is not to judge individual alerts. The records are evidence of whether a CSE is actually capable in eight areas: threat detection, investigation, escalation, incident response, security operations, governance and oversight, operational discipline, and cyber resilience. Manual review works, but it does not scale to more entities and more data. NCIIPC wants a tool that helps examiners find the entities and records that need attention, explains why, and runs fully offline. It must not turn into a SOC, a SIEM or a monitoring platform.

**What we propose.** An air-gapped analytics workstation. It ingests periodic SOC submissions in the formats CSEs already have: CSV, JSON, NDJSON, SQLite database exports, loopback REST endpoints, and exports from Splunk ES, ServiceNow SIR and TheHive 5. It runs 20 deterministic detection rules, a cross-entity systemic check and an exploratory anomaly scan. It then scores each entity across the eight capability areas and gives the examiner a prioritised review queue with evidence.

**Who uses it.** NCIIPC Analysts run ingestion, assessments and rule calibration. NCIIPC Examiners review the findings, make the decisions and issue dossiers. The CSEs themselves benefit indirectly: power, banking, telecom, transport, oil and gas and others get their SOC weaknesses found before an attacker finds them.

**Built with.** Python 3.11+, DuckDB over Parquet for analytics, SQLite (WAL mode) for state and the audit log, FastAPI with server-rendered Jinja2 pages, Apache ECharts shipped inside the app, ReportLab for PDFs, Polars and PyArrow, a Typer command line, and the `cryptography` library for signed audit checkpoints. There is no machine learning and no language model anywhere in it.

## Section 1: Problem Analysis

The volume of cyber incidents in India has grown far faster than any manual review can follow. CERT-In handled 15,92,917 incidents in 2023, up from 3,94,499 in 2019. Incidents involving government organisations rose from 85,797 to 2,04,844 over the same years (figures presented to the Lok Sabha). Each incident that reaches a critical sector entity goes through that entity's SOC first. Whether the SOC actually detects, investigates and escalates it is exactly what NCIIPC needs to know.

### Why a SOC can look fine and not be

A SOC can report healthy numbers while the real work slips. Public figures show how big that gap can get.

Analysts are overwhelmed. In Vectra's 2023 survey of 2,000 security analysts, SOC teams received 4,484 alerts a day on average, could not get to 67% of them, and spent 2.7 hours a day on manual triage. When two-thirds of alerts go unhandled, quick closures, copy-pasted comments and silent assets follow.

Late detection is expensive. IBM's 2025 report puts the average cost of a data breach in India at INR 220 million, 13% more than in 2024, with an average of 263 days to identify and contain one. Transportation, one of NCIIPC's sectors, averaged INR 288 million.

And supervision is still manual. A sample drawn at random from thousands of alerts rarely lands on the handful of records that show a real weakness.

### What current approaches miss

1. **Nobody checks the declared KPIs.** A CSE reports its mean time to respond, and nothing recomputes it from the records it submitted. A declared 96 minutes and a real 9 hours look the same on a questionnaire.
2. **A sample cannot show what is missing.** The worst failures leave no trace: a critical server that sent no logs for days, an alert type every peer sees but this entity never raises, a critical incident never reported outside. You cannot sample your way to an absence.
3. **Some patterns only show across the whole population.** One analyst closing 30 alerts in an hour, a quarter of closures landing just before the SLA deadline, the same comment pasted on hundreds of closures. None of this is visible in 25 records.
4. **There is no peer baseline.** Whether a 98% false-positive rate is normal depends on the sector and the size of the entity. Examiners have nothing to compare against.
5. **Commercial tools solve a different problem.** SIEM and SOAR products run a SOC from the inside. They do not assess one from the outside, and cloud analytics or hosted AI are ruled out by the problem statement anyway.
6. **Findings have to hold up.** A finding that cites no records, or that came out of a black-box model, cannot be put to a CSE and defended.

### How big the problem is

It covers every critical sector NCIIPC is responsible for. Each entity submits thousands to hundreds of thousands of alerts per period, along with cases, workflow events, escalations, closures and asset inventories. We have measured SAT-SA at 100,000 alerts for each of 50 entities. Against that, an examiner reads a few dozen records per entity, entity after entity, period after period, with no guarantee that any of them shows a problem.

Two numbers we could not find in public sources are the number of designated CSEs and the examiner hours a review takes today. We would confirm both with NCIIPC in the pilot.

So the issue is not missing data. The CSEs already hold every record needed to show whether their SOC works. What is missing is a way to read all of it offline, consistently, and point the examiner at the records that matter.

## Section 2: Proposed Solution and Innovation

SAT-SA reads everything an entity submits and puts the most telling records in front of the examiner first.

### The main idea: sample by evidence, not at random

Instead of a random sample, the examiner gets a review queue built from what the rules found, topped up with a random control sample (stratified by severity) so that blind spots in the rules can still be measured. On our primary validation dataset, the first 25 alerts in the queue held 13 defect-affected records. A random sample of 25 would be expected to hold fewer than one, since only 2.67% of alerts are affected. That is a lift of 19.5 times. Across the whole 130-alert queue the lift is 5.19 times. The data is synthetic, and the shadow pilot in Section 4 is how we would get the real figure.

Four design decisions make this work.

- **We look for what is missing.** Eight of the 20 rules test for evidence that should be in a submission and is not.
- **Every result can be reproduced.** Each rule is a SQL query with named, configurable thresholds. The same submission and the same rule pack always give the same findings, so a CSE can be shown exactly why it was flagged. Because there is no AI or machine learning, the problem statement's AI/ML disclosure clause ("where AI or machine learning is proposed") does not apply.
- **We assess from outside the SOC.** SAT-SA never connects to a CSE's systems. It works on the periodic batch the CSE exports and accepts one batch per entity per period. That keeps it clear of all six out-of-scope items: it is not a SOC and does not replace one, does no real-time monitoring, is not a SIEM, is not a central SOC for several entities, does not collect logs or telemetry continuously, and is not a national monitoring platform.
- **The examiner decides.** The tool ranks and explains. Every item in the queue waits for a human decision, scores can be hidden until the examiner has made up their own mind, and every output says it is an indicator for supervisory review, not a compliance determination.

### What it does

**1. Finds execution gaps (EG01 to EG12).** These rules check whether the recorded work matches the reported work. They flag:
- high or critical alerts closed faster than 95% of the peer group's, with at most one workflow step (EG01)
- alerts closed with no investigation step and a token comment (EG02)
- critical true positives never escalated (EG03)
- the same closing comment reused across a large share of closures (EG04)
- the same alert on the same asset repeatedly closed as benign, with no remediation ticket (EG05)
- bulk closures, or more than 25% of closures landing in the last 10% of the SLA window (EG06)
- 30 or more closures by one analyst within a single hour (EG07)
- escalations Tier 2 never picked up (EG08)
- cases left open too long (EG09)
- a response time, recomputed from the alert timestamps, more than 60% above what the entity declared (EG10)
- implausible disposition rates (EG11)
- critical cases that skipped containment (EG12)

**2. Finds negative space (NS01 to NS08).** These rules look for what should be there and is not:
- critical assets with no log events on 3 or more days (NS01), judged from the daily log counts per asset that come with the batch; SAT-SA never collects logs itself
- alert categories that 60% of peers report and this entity never does (NS02)
- night-time activity far below peers (NS03)
- high or critical true positives with no investigation case (NS04)
- detection rules that are switched on but never fire (NS05)
- inventory assets with no logs and no alerts (NS06)
- critical cases with no external report (NS07)
- months missing from the submission (NS08)

**3. Compares entities with their peers.** Entities are grouped by sector and size, with a broader fallback group when fewer than 3 peers are comparable. Four rules judge against the peer group, two of them by robust z-score (median and MAD). An anomaly scan compares about 40 operational rates per entity with its peers and flags any at |z| ≥ 3.5, with at least 3 peers and 30 records behind each value. It also looks for sudden shifts over time, using CUSUM against the entity's first three months. The rates include workload measures: the busiest analyst's share of closures, analysts per 1,000 closures, workflow events per alert, and escalations per true positive. That lets it surface warning signs no rule was written for, which is what the problem statement means by "previously unknown" indicators. Anomaly leads are shown separately and never change a score.

**4. Produces a risk index.** Rule scores roll up into the eight capability areas and a 0–100 risk index per entity. When 3 or more entities that share one SOC provider trip the same rule, SAT-SA raises a single systemic finding against the provider.

**5. Prioritises entities, controls, processes and alerts.** Entities are ranked by risk index. Controls (rules) are ranked by how many entities failed them, then by severity. Processes (the eight areas) are ranked by how many entities score 50 or more. Each entity's review queue is filled up to 70% with records the findings cite, highest score first, and the rest with a random control sample. For each item the examiner chooses Escalate to Statutory Notice, Mark as Justified, or Request CSE Explanation.

**6. Explains every finding and records every action.** A finding page says what was found, why it matters, which records it rests on, the thresholds and confidence involved, the innocent explanations to rule out, and what to do next. Every ingest, run, threshold change, user change and decision goes into a hash-chained audit log. The examiner sees a portfolio league table, a heatmap, a trend chart across periods, an entity radar against the peer median and declared-versus-recomputed KPIs, and can drill down from any finding to the underlying records. Dossiers come out as PDF, HTML and CSV.

Between them, these features answer all 17 functional requirements in the problem statement. The requirement-by-requirement mapping to code and tests is in `docs/ps_traceability.md`.

### Every example in the problem statement has a named check

| Example from the problem statement | SAT-SA check |

| Cases, or high-severity alerts, closed unusually quickly | EG01 |
| Critical alerts closed without escalation | EG03 |
| Repetitive or template-driven investigations | EG04 |

The problem statement also invites signals beyond its examples. Ours are declared-KPI reconciliation (EG10), shared-vendor systemic findings, submission completeness (NS08), case backlogs (EG09), skipped containment on critical cases (EG12), and per-entity data-quality defects found at ingest.

### From submission to decision

1. The CSE exports its batch for the period from Splunk ES, ServiceNow SIR, TheHive 5, a SQLite database, or as plain CSV or JSON.
2. The Analyst ingests it. SAT-SA maps the fields, replaces analyst and case-owner names with pseudonyms, masks sensitive text, and writes a data-quality report. The report includes the rules this submission cannot support.
3. The Analyst runs the assessment. Rules, systemic check and anomaly scan run, scores are calculated, and the review queue is built.
4. The Examiner works from the portfolio down: league table, entity profile, finding, records.
5. The Examiner decides each queue item.
6. The CSE receives a PDF dossier or a request for explanation.

The audit log records every step, so it is always clear who prepared what and who decided what. Duties are separated. The Analyst who ingests data and tunes thresholds cannot record decisions, and the Examiner cannot change the rules. Administrators create both kinds of account on a separate portal, and nobody can sign themselves up.

### Fitting in with what exists

- **CSEs change nothing.** They export what their tools already hold. Product mappings translate Splunk ES notable events, ServiceNow security incidents and TheHive 5 alerts and cases into one common model. Anything else arrives as CSV, JSON, a SQLite file or a ZIP. If a table is missing, the ingest report lists the rules that could not be assessed. A gap in the data is never counted as a SOC failure.
- **It follows NCIIPC's review cycle.** One batch per entity per period, by upload or a local API, suits a quarterly or half-yearly calendar. A second batch for the same period is refused.
- **It produces the paperwork examiners need.** PDF and HTML dossiers, CSV exports and finding pages all carry the notice "Indicators requiring supervisory review; not a compliance determination".
- **It suits a secure room.** One ordinary workstation, no network. Threshold changes travel as signed rule packs on removable media.

### Three illustrative cases

Each case is a pattern a rule catches in our synthetic validation data, told as it might look in a real portfolio.

**A power utility with a silent substation.** A grid operator's submission shows four of its most critical assets with no log events on 5 separate days. NS01 raises a critical finding listing the assets and dates, and the finding page suggests asking whether a log forwarder was down or an asset was retired. No sample of alerts could show this, because a silent asset raises no alerts.

**A bank whose response time looks too good.** A bank declares a 51-minute mean time to respond for critical alerts. SAT-SA recomputes it from the submitted timestamps, finds it more than 60% higher, and EG10 raises a critical finding. EG06 adds that 31% of closures land in the last 10% of the SLA window. The examiner starts with the alerts those findings cite.

**Three telecom operators, one SOC provider.** Three entities that outsource to the same managed SOC all trip EG04 (copy-pasted closures) in the same run. SAT-SA raises one systemic finding, "possible shared-vendor issue", so NCIIPC can take it up with the provider once instead of chasing three entities separately.

## Section 3: Technical Implementation

SAT-SA is one Python application on one offline workstation. Bulk records go into a columnar analytics engine. Findings and decisions go into a separate tamper-evident ledger. Two browser portals run on the local machine. We chose each component against three tests from the problem statement: it has to work with no network, it has to give the same answer every time, and an examiner has to be able to see why.

### Why we chose what we chose

**DuckDB over Parquet** runs inside the application, so there is no database server to install, patch or secure. On a 4-core laptop, 50 entities and 5,000,000 alerts (21.4 million rows across all tables) were ingested in 46.6 seconds and assessed in 124 seconds. Assessment time varied between 106 and 191 seconds across runs (measured 2 October 2026). A client-server database would have added an installation, a network listener and a password to an air-gapped room.

**SQLite in WAL mode** holds runs, findings, scores, decisions and the audit log in a single transactional file. Keeping it apart from the analytics store means bulk data can be re-ingested without touching the evidence trail.

**Polars and PyArrow** let ingest process 100,000 rows at a time in columnar form. Together with per-column processing, this took ingesting 5,000,000 alerts from 603 seconds and a 13,248 MiB memory peak (30 September) to 46.6 seconds and 5,425 MiB (2 October).

**FastAPI with server-rendered Jinja2** gives typed request validation and automatic API documentation. Pages are rendered on the server, so the browser needs no build step and loads no outside script.

**Apache ECharts** ships inside the package, so radar, heatmap and trend charts never call a CDN. **ReportLab** builds PDF dossiers locally, with the supervisory notice on every page. **Typer and Rich** put every step on the command line (generate, ingest, run, validate, report, audit verify, audit checkpoint, rules export), so any run can be scripted and repeated.

For installation there is an offline bundle (CI installs it with no network access), a Windows start script, and a Dockerfile with a compose file.

**Why no machine learning?** The problem statement wants every finding explained and allows no externally hosted model. A trained model would need labelled data NCIIPC does not yet have, would drift from one period to the next, and could not be given to a CSE as a reason. So we use SQL rules, robust statistics (median, MAD, CUSUM) and fixed text templates. None of the 14 runtime dependencies is a machine-learning or language-model library.

### Architecture

Everything runs on one workstation that listens only on the local machine and makes no outbound connections. There are four layers.

1. **Ingest** maps a batch into six record types, pseudonymises names, masks sensitive text and checks data quality.
2. **Storage** puts bulk records in Parquet for DuckDB, and every event, finding and decision in the SQLite ledger.
3. **Analysis** resolves peer groups, runs the 20 rules, the systemic check and the anomaly scan as SQL, scores the results and builds the queue.
4. **Serving** drives the two portals, the reports and the API.

The NCIIPC Administration Portal manages users, organisations and the CSE registry, and shows a feed of operator activity. The SAT-SA Supervisory Tool is where Analysts and Examiners work. Both bind to 127.0.0.1 by default.

### How data moves through it

1. **Input.** CSV, JSON or NDJSON files, a SQLite export, a ZIP bundle, or a Splunk ES, ServiceNow SIR or TheHive 5 export (translated by `satsa ingest --source`). It arrives by browser upload, the command line, a loopback REST endpoint (`satsa ingest --api-config`), or `POST /api/v1/submissions`, which takes one batch per entity per period and refuses a repeat with HTTP 409.
2. **Mapping.** A YAML mapping per source renames product fields into six record types: alert, case, workflow event, escalation, closure and asset. A taxonomy step aligns severities and categories. Any file the mapping does not describe is reported, not guessed at.
3. **Privacy.** Analyst and case-owner names become HMAC-SHA256 pseudonyms keyed by a local secret. IP addresses, emails, hostnames and long numbers are masked. Closure comments are never stored as text. Only a normalised hash, the length and hashes of three-word fragments are kept, which is enough to spot copy-paste templates.
4. **Data quality.** Required fields, closures before creation, duplicate IDs, gaps in alert-ID sequences, null rates and orphaned references are all checked. A manifest records which tables each entity sent, so a rule whose input never arrived is reported as "not assessed".
5. **Storage.** Records are written to Parquet, partitioned by table and entity, and the ingest is added to the audit chain.
6. **Peer groups.** Each entity goes into a group by sector and size, falling back to a broader group when fewer than 3 peers are comparable.
7. **Rules.** The 20 rules run as SQL. Rules that depend on age measure against a reference date stored with the run, never the system clock, so re-running next month gives the same answer.
8. **Scoring.** A rule's score reflects how far past its threshold the entity is, weighted by severity and by how much data supports it. Within each capability area the scores combine as a noisy-OR: area score = 100 × (1 − the product of (1 − rule score ÷ 100)). Several moderate failures therefore add up, without any single rule dominating. The risk index is 90% the weighted average of the areas and 10% the number of distinct rules tripped. Threat detection, investigation, escalation, incident response, and governance and oversight weigh 15% each; security operations 10%; operational discipline and cyber resilience 7.5% each. An entity with a critical finding is never placed below the High band, so one serious problem cannot hide in an average. Moving every area weight by ±20% left the entity ranking unchanged (Spearman ρ = 1.0).
9. **Prioritisation.** Entities, controls, processes and queue items are ranked as described in Section 2.
10. **Output.** League table and trend chart, entity profiles, finding pages with drill-down, anomaly leads, the review queue, PDF, HTML and CSV dossiers, and a JSON API.

### API

The API is small, local, and connects to nothing outside the machine.
- **Batches in:** `POST /api/v1/submissions`.
- **Results out (read-only):** `GET /api/v1/entities`, `/findings`, `/anomalies`, `/priorities`, `/queue` and `/runs`.
- **Examiner decisions in:** `POST /api/v1/feedback`.
- **Audit and export:** `GET /api/v1/audit/verify` rechecks the hash chain, and `GET /api/v1/export/queue.csv` exports the queue for working papers.

Product data comes in only as file exports. No single export supports all 20 rules: Splunk ES alone supports 8, ServiceNow SIR 2, TheHive 5 alone 5, and Splunk and ServiceNow together 10. The ingest report says which rules each submission could and could not assess. The remaining tables (assets, escalations, declared KPIs) are submitted alongside in the common layout.

### Security and privacy

- **The air gap is enforced.** All web assets ship with the app, outbound Python network connections are blocked at runtime, and a test opens every page under a socket guard to prove nothing leaves the machine. The guard is a second line of defence; it cannot stop native code that bypasses Python's sockets.
- **The audit log is tamper-evident.** Each entry carries the SHA3-256 hash of the previous one, so editing, inserting, deleting or reordering an entry breaks verification. Deleting the newest entries is caught by comparing against a checkpoint kept off the machine and signed with Ed25519 (`satsa audit checkpoint --sign`). It is tamper-evident, not tamper-proof.
- **Access is controlled.** Nobody self-registers. Passphrases are stored with PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 at 200,000 iterations. Five failed sign-ins lock an account for 15 minutes, and every default account must change its passphrase at first sign-in. Analysts, Examiners and Administrators are each refused the others' functions (HTTP 403), and a test checks every role against every route.
- **Rule packs are signed.** Threshold changes export with SHA-256 checksums and an HMAC signature, and import checks both before writing anything.
- **We keep data to a minimum.** SAT-SA works on alert and case metadata, not raw logs or packet captures, as the problem statement asks. A test scans every stored file and table for personal identifiers from the sample exports.
- **TLS is optional.** A locally generated RSA-3072 certificate is available for when the portals must be reached from another machine on an isolated network.
- **We are careful with legal claims.** Every page, PDF page and exported record carries the supervisory notice. How the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 applies, and the statutory basis for findings, are marked for legal review rather than assumed.

### Interface

The interface is designed around the examiner's decision. Each page answers one question:
- Which entities need attention? League table, heatmap and trend.
- Why this one? Radar against the peer median, declared versus recomputed KPIs.
- What exactly happened? Finding page with reasons, thresholds and records.
- What should I look at first? The review queue.

Pages are rendered on the server, work at tablet width, and come in light and dark themes. A blinded review mode hides scores until the examiner has recorded their own view, so they do not simply anchor on the tool's number. Users only see the controls their role allows.

### How we know it works

- **Tests:** 1,136 automated tests pass, with none failing (4 October 2026). They include property-based tests. Coverage is 89.3% of statements and branches, and 94–100% for the rules.
- **Lint and types:** ruff and mypy pass clean across 73 source files.
- **CI:** GitHub Actions runs the suite, both validation sets, audit verification, linting and type checks on Ubuntu with Python 3.11 and 3.13. A separate job installs the offline bundle with no network.
- **Validation data:** two separately written synthetic data generators plant known defects and near-miss decoys, so each rule's accuracy can be measured before real data exists.
- **Shadow pilot:** a shadow-pilot mode (`satsa validate --shadow-csv`) scores SAT-SA against examiner workpapers once NCIIPC can share them.

## Section 4: Feasibility and Impact

The prototype already works end to end. Every one of the 17 functional requirements and the 6 deployment requirements is mapped to code and tests, and the demo data goes from upload to signed dossier. So we will spend the 36 hours on the things we have written down as still open, on testing the tool against the evaluators' data, and on rehearsing the examiner's journey.

### The 36 hours

The first two hours set the baseline. We install the offline bundle on the demo machine, run the full test suite and all three validation sets, and freeze and hash the rule pack. Six streams of work then run in parallel. The full suite and validation run at hours 12 and 24, and nothing is merged if either fails. Code freezes at hour 30. We use hours 30 to 34 for the 5,000,000-alert scale run (about 3 minutes) and demo rehearsal, and keep hours 34 to 36 as buffer for submission.

- **Connectors.** Map any sample export the evaluators provide, and finish a fourth product mapping (Cortex XSIAM case exports), which is under way.
- **Analytics.** Recalibrate EG05's repeat-pair threshold, the only rule with false positives on our stress set. Decide the two threshold proposals we have documented (EG04's group size scaled with volume, EG11's spread floor), and re-run the ±20% sensitivity sweep.
- **Platform.** Build and run the Docker image, which we have not yet done. Pre-warm the analytics cache after each run, so the first page after a 5,000,000-alert run no longer takes 17 seconds. Repeat the offline install on a physically disconnected machine.
- **Interface.** Run our written usability protocol with two or three stand-in examiners, record the first usability figures, and fix what they find.
- **Validation.** After every merge, run the hard set, the stress set over 20 seeds and the independent generator. Run the shadow-pilot mode on any workpaper sample the evaluators can share.
- **Pitch and traceability.** Re-check every quoted requirement against the problem statement, and prepare its evaluation deliverables: a 2-page architecture document, a 2-minute demo video and a 5-slide presentation.

### What we need

- **Data.** Our two synthetic generators (any seed, measured up to 100,000 alerts per entity), three demo upload bundles covering January to June 2026, sample Splunk ES, ServiceNow SIR and TheHive 5 exports, and any anonymised CSE sample the evaluators can share.
- **External APIs.** None.
- **Hardware.** A laptop per developer, and one demo workstation with 16 GB of RAM (8 GB is enough for the demo dataset).
- **Software.** Python 3.11+, uv, Docker, pytest, ruff, mypy and Git. All open source, no licences.

### What changes, in numbers

All SAT-SA figures here were measured on the prototype. Accuracy figures are from synthetic data.

- **The right records come first.** In the first 25 alerts an examiner reads, defect-affected records go from about 0.7 (random sample, 2.67% base rate) to 13 (SAT-SA's queue).
- **Everything gets read.** Coverage goes from a few dozen sampled records per entity to every submitted record, under every rule, every period.
- **It scales.** 50 entities and 5,000,000 alerts were ingested and assessed in under 3 minutes on a 4-core laptop. Repeat page loads stayed under half a second at that size.
- **KPIs get checked.** Declared response times are recomputed from the records for every entity, every period.
- **Absence gets checked.** Eight rules test for missing evidence that no sample can show.
- **It catches what we plant, on synthetic data.** It found all 503 defects planted across 24 synthetic portfolio runs at three volumes, with no false findings and none of the 72 clean entities flagged. Precision was 93.8% on a deliberately ambiguous stress set. On a second generator written separately, it found all 487 planted defects and flagged none of the 245 decoys set just under a threshold. We wrote both generators knowing the rules, so these results show the code does what its specification says, not how it will do on real data.
- **The answer does not depend on who asks.** Every run records its reference date and configuration hash, so the same input always gives the same result.

How many examiner hours this saves per entity is something we will measure in the pilot against NCIIPC's current review time. We have not estimated it.

### Validating against expert manual review

The problem statement asks how the tool will be checked against findings from expert manual review, and asks for results "comparable to or better than current manual sampling approaches". Our shadow pilot (written runbook: `docs/shadow_pilot_runbook.md`) covers both.

1. NCIIPC picks a past review period for which it holds the submissions and the examiners' workpapers.
2. We freeze the rule pack and record its hash before anyone on our side opens a workpaper.
3. SAT-SA assesses that period. `satsa validate --shadow-csv` scores the results against the examiners' findings. It reports recall and precision with 95% confidence intervals, and how many of the examiner-confirmed records the queue put in front of the examiner.
4. "Comparable to or better than manual sampling" is measured as defects found per record reviewed: SAT-SA's queue against a random sample of the same size from the same data.
5. Blinded review shows how often an examiner who forms a view before seeing the score agrees with the tool. That checks the quality of supervision, not just its speed.

We have run this pipeline end to end on a stand-in workpaper built from synthetic ground truth. That shows the method works. It is not evidence of real-world accuracy.

| Detecting execution gaps | 12 rules (EG01–EG12); all planted defects found in 24 synthetic runs |
| Detecting negative space | 8 rules (NS01–NS08); missing tables reported as "not assessed", never as defects |
| Explainability and auditability | Finding pages with thresholds and records; hash-chained audit log with signed checkpoints; reproducible runs |
| Scalability and performance | 50 entities, 5,000,000 alerts, under 3 minutes on a laptop |
| Innovation and additional insights | Evidence-led sampling (19.5x lift), KPI reconciliation, shared-vendor findings, anomaly leads, blinded review |

### Risks and how we handle them

- **No real CSE data has been through SAT-SA yet**, so real-world accuracy is unknown. The shadow pilot above is the answer, with rules frozen before any workpaper is opened.
- **The product connectors were tested on sample exports we built**, not on live systems. A pilot with real exports from two or three CSEs will test them. Meanwhile the ingest report lists every rule a submission could not support.
- **No single product export supports all 20 rules.** The best pair supports 10. Common-format templates cover the missing tables, and missing input is reported as "not assessed", never as a defect.
- **Some thresholds are sensitive.** A 20% change shifts EG05's result, and it flagged a noisy clean entity in 4 of 20 stress runs. We will calibrate thresholds on pilot data, ship them as signed rule packs, and run the ±20% sweep before every release.
- **The anomaly scan is unproven.** Its leads are shown separately and never scored until the pilot measures their false-positive rate.
- **A CSE could falsify timestamps consistently across all its systems.** SAT-SA cross-checks independent records, but it does not replace an on-site inspection. We state this as a limit.
- **The legal standing of findings is not settled.** Every output carries the supervisory notice. We have listed the open points for NCIIPC's legal review: Section 70A of the IT Act, the DPDP Act, the reporting duties behind NS07, and the wording of the "Escalate to Statutory Notice" option.

### After the hackathon

1. **Shadow pilot (months 1–3).** Run the frozen rules over one past review period and compare with the examiners' workpapers. We move on once we have published recall and precision against real findings, with confidence intervals.
2. **Sector pilot (months 4–6).** Bring 5 to 10 CSEs in one sector onto live periodic submissions and calibrate thresholds. We move on once examiner hours per entity are measured against today and a calibrated rule pack is signed off.
3. **All sectors (months 7–12).** Extend to every designated sector, with peer groups by sector and size, and train Analysts and Examiners. We move on once legal review of the outputs is complete and operating procedures are adopted.
4. **Steady state (year 2 onwards).** Trends across successive real submissions, an anonymised sector-wide benchmark, shared-provider findings across sectors, and an annual rule-pack review. Throughout, SAT-SA stays a periodic supervisory tool and never becomes a monitoring platform.

### Costs and benefits

- **Hardware.** One ordinary workstation per examination station. Our full benchmark ran on a 4-core laptop.
- **Licences and cloud.** Zero. Every component is open source and nothing runs online.
- **Running costs.** A small team to maintain rule packs, connectors and releases, plus Analyst time each cycle. We will measure these in the sector pilot.
- **Benefit.** Examiners read the most important records first, every record gets checked every period, and declared KPIs are verified instead of taken on trust. Preventing, or containing earlier, one breach at one CSE avoids losses on the scale of the INR 220 million Indian average. That is many times the cost of the workstation and the team.

### Precedents

- **RBI's DAKSH (October 2022).** The Reserve Bank of India already supervises regulated entities through a supervisory technology platform, including cyber incident reporting and analysis. SAT-SA brings the same approach to SOC operations in critical infrastructure.
- **Supervisory technology elsewhere.** The BIS Financial Stability Institute's "Peering through the hype: assessing suptech tools' transition from experimentation to supervision" (FSI Insights No 58, June 2024) looks at how supervisors move tools like this from trials into everyday use. That is the path our shadow and sector pilots follow.

SAT-SA turns NCIIPC's sample review into evidence-led supervision: every record read, the most telling ones first, every finding explained, and every decision recorded. One shadow pilot on a past review period is enough to measure how much examiner time it saves and how many SOC weaknesses it catches.
